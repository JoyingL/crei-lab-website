About

A geotechnical lab at Tufts CEE working on climate-resilient and equitable infrastructure. The lab combines fundamental soil mechanics with applied work alongside communities and infrastructure partners.

Challenge

Climate is changing the loading on infrastructure faster than much of that infrastructure was designed to accept. Drought, flooding, wildfire, and compound or cascading sequences do not stay in the atmosphere. They wet, dry, heat, and weaken the soils that hold slopes, dams, and levees, then cascade into the communities those systems are meant to protect. The people most exposed are often the least represented in the original design. Treating climate as a new load case while leaving equity to another chapter misses both the mechanics and the harm.

Contribution

The lab works at the interface of civil and geotechnical engineering with climate change, resilient infrastructure, and environmental justice. The focus is the resilience and adaptation of critical infrastructure to extreme events in a changing climate, with particular attention to disadvantaged communities. That is a lab problem, not a personal brand: show how wetting, drying, and heat change the performance of earthen systems, and keep who sits behind those systems inside the analysis. Support has included NSF, USDA, NOAA, NASA, and DOD, among others.

Approach

Fundamental work starts from variably saturated soils and earthen structures under multi-physics processes: flow, heat, and stress kept in one analysis rather than added later as a correction. Applied work takes the same mechanics to climate-loaded slopes, dams, and levees. Collaboration with climate science, hydrology, geosciences, social science, remote sensing, and data science is part of the method because the failure sequence does not stay inside one department. The lab sits in Civil and Environmental Engineering at Tufts.

Principal investigator

Farshid Vahedifard is Professor of Civil and Environmental Engineering at Tufts and the Louis Berger Chair. His research sits at the interface of geotechnical engineering, climate, and environmental justice, with emphasis on how extreme events affect infrastructure and disadvantaged communities.
